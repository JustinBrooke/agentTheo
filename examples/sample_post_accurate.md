# The Real Presence: Catholic Teaching on the Eucharist

The Eucharist is the "source and summit of the Christian life" (Lumen Gentium 11). In this post, I'll explain what the Catholic Church truly teaches about this great mystery.

## Transubstantiation

The Catholic Church teaches that at the words of consecration, the bread and wine truly become the Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity of Jesus Christ. This is called transubstantiation.

As the Catechism states:

> "In the most blessed sacrament of the Eucharist 'the body and blood, together with the soul and divinity, of our Lord Jesus Christ and, therefore, the whole Christ is truly, really, and substantially contained.'" (CCC 1374)

This is not symbolic or figurative, but a substantial change. The appearances (accidents) of bread and wine remain, but the substance is wholly changed into Christ.

## Scriptural Foundation

Our Lord's words are clear: "This IS my body" (Matthew 26:26). In John 6, Jesus insists: "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you" (John 6:53).

When many disciples left because this teaching was too hard, Jesus did not call them back to explain it was merely symbolic. Instead, he asked the Twelve: "Do you also want to leave?" (John 6:67)

## Witness of the Church Fathers

From the earliest times, the Church believed in the Real Presence:

- St. Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110 AD) wrote against those who "abstain from the Eucharist... because they do not confess that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Savior Jesus Christ."

- St. Justin Martyr (c. 150 AD): "We do not receive these things as common bread or common drink... but as the flesh and blood of that incarnate Jesus."

- St. Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350 AD): "Do not, therefore, regard the bread and wine as simply that; for they are, according to the Master's declaration, the body and blood of Christ."

## Conclusion

The Real Presence is not optional Catholic piety but defined dogma. The Council of Trent solemnly declared this truth, and it has been consistently taught from apostolic times to the present day.
